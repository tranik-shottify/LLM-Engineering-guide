PART 13

Trends to watch (2025-2026)

Where the engineering frontier is actually moving — distinguishing durable shifts from cycle noise.

This part is a deliberately opinionated map of where applied LLM engineering is heading over the next eighteen months. The aim is to separate the trends that will reshape system design from the ones that look loud on social media but settle into normal incremental progress. Read it as a compass, not a forecast — the dates are approximate and the punch lines are what matter.

Agentic AI graduating from demos to production

For most of 2023 and 2024 the term agent meant a loop of "LLM picks a tool, observes the result, decides again". The demos were viral; the production deployments were scarce. What changed in 2025 is that the supporting machinery — typed tool schemas, durable execution, structured planning, deterministic replay, and per-step evaluation — finally arrived in roughly compatible form across vendors and open-source frameworks. Agents are now being used in narrow, high-leverage roles: code modification with pull-request-grade review, customer-support triage with bounded actions, internal knowledge work where a human is always the final approver, and DevOps remediation against well-defined runbooks.

The durable design lessons from this transition are mostly negative: open-ended ReAct loops are unstable past five or six steps; success rates on multi-step tasks are still well under what marketing materials suggest; and the dominant failure mode is not bad reasoning but bad tools — schemas that allow ambiguous calls, error messages the model cannot recover from, and side effects that cannot be undone. Production agents in 2026 look less like autonomous explorers and more like state machines with an LLM in the dispatch slot, surrounded by retries, idempotency keys, budget caps, and human-in-the-loop checkpoints.

Senior tip

If your agent's average trajectory length is greater than ten steps, you almost certainly have an under-specified tool surface. Look for the tool that the model keeps calling and re-calling — that is the one whose return shape is wrong, not whose docstring needs more adjectives.

Computer-use and browser-control agents

Computer-use agents — models that operate a screen, mouse, and keyboard, or drive a real browser through DOM and accessibility APIs — moved from research demos into shipped products during 2025. The current generation is competent at well-scoped tasks (filling forms, extracting structured data from a workflow that has no public API, navigating expense systems, scheduling) and brittle at long-horizon work. The genuinely interesting engineering question is no longer "can the model click the right button" but "how do we build a substrate around it that is observable, sandboxed, reversible, and auditable when it gets things wrong."

Practitioners adopting browser agents in 2026 should design for three properties: (a) a recorded trace of every action with screenshots and DOM snapshots so failures can be replayed; (b) a permissioned action layer where destructive operations require either a typed confirmation step or an out-of-band approval; and (c) deterministic shadow mode, where the agent's intended actions are logged but not executed, used as the default during onboarding for any new workflow.

MCP and the rise of open tool protocols

The Model Context Protocol — a JSON-RPC-based open standard for exposing tools, resources, and prompts to LLM-driven applications — went from a single-vendor proposal in late 2024 to a broadly supported ecosystem during 2025. Its importance is not technical novelty (the protocol is intentionally boring) but coordination: it means an organization can write a server once and have it work in multiple host applications without bespoke integration glue. For internal platforms, MCP is rapidly becoming the default way to wrap line-of-business systems for LLM consumption, much as REST became the default integration layer in the 2010s.

Adjacent standards are emerging around the edges: streaming structured outputs, tool-call result schemas, agent-to-agent delegation, and evaluation manifests. None is dominant yet. The bet to make in 2026 is not on a particular standard but on the broader principle: prefer protocols you can read in an afternoon over SDKs you cannot replace.

Long context (1M+ tokens) and the retrieval trade-off

Million-token contexts are now widely available, and several frontier models accept inputs of two million tokens or more. For a class of problems — codebase comprehension, long-document analysis, repeated few-shot reasoning over a fixed corpus — this changes the architecture: the question is no longer "how do I retrieve the right ten chunks" but "do I need to retrieve at all". The honest answer is sometimes, and the deciding factors are predictable.

Long context wins when the working set fits comfortably below the model's effective attention horizon (which is usually shorter than the advertised window), when the cost of carrying the whole context is amortized via prompt caching across many requests, and when the corpus is small enough that retrieval errors dominate. RAG wins when the corpus is large or changes frequently, when freshness matters within minutes, when the same context will not be reused, and when explainability requires citing specific source passages. Hybrid is the most common shape in 2026: retrieval narrows a multi-million-token corpus to a hundred-thousand-token working set, which the model then reasons over with full attention.

Key idea

"Long context replaces RAG" is the wrong framing. Long context replaces chunking and reranking when the corpus is small; RAG remains the right tool whenever the corpus is large, fresh, or governed.

Test-time compute as a deployment knob

The reasoning-model wave that began in late 2024 made the inference-time compute budget a first-class deployment parameter. A model can now be asked to spend one token, ten thousand tokens, or a million tokens of internal deliberation before producing a final answer; the trade-off between latency, cost, and answer quality is something the application chooses, not something hard-coded by the model vendor.

The practical consequence is that production routing layers in 2026 increasingly look like a small dispatcher: a fast classifier (or just a heuristic) decides whether the incoming request needs deep reasoning, shallow reasoning, or none at all, and then chooses the appropriate model and reasoning budget. Treating this as a tunable rather than a property of the model is what separates teams that ship reasoning-capable products on a sustainable cost curve from those that bankrupt themselves on a single fascinating demo.

On-device and edge LLMs

Models in the one-to-eight-billion-parameter range, aggressively quantized, now run usefully on phones, laptops, and modest single-GPU desktops. This unlocks a privacy-first design pattern: the small model handles routing, intent extraction, on-device personalization, and the easy fraction of requests; only the residual hard requests are forwarded to a cloud frontier model. The architecture is reminiscent of CDN edge caching from a previous era, and the same operational lessons apply — versioning the edge artifact is a real release process, not a side concern.

The interesting open question is the user-experience design pattern. Apps that show users when they are running locally versus in the cloud (and what that means for privacy and quality) are starting to outperform apps that hide the distinction. Expect this transparency to become an accepted convention by the end of 2026.

Synthetic-data flywheels and self-improvement

Synthetic data — model-generated training data, filtered through reward models or rule-based verifiers — is now the dominant source of fine-tuning data for many post-training pipelines. The flywheel pattern works: a strong model generates candidate completions, a verifier (often a smaller specialized model, sometimes a deterministic checker) keeps the good ones, and the resulting dataset trains the next iteration. Math and code are the cleanest cases because verification is cheap and unambiguous; natural-language tasks remain harder because the verifier is itself an LLM and inherits its biases.

The risk is well-documented and worth restating: uncritical recursive training on model-generated data leads to mode collapse and quality regression. The best practice in 2026 mixes synthetic data with a smaller volume of carefully curated human data on every iteration, treats the verifier as the most important component (not the generator), and tracks distributional diversity metrics on the training corpus over time.

Hardware shifts and what they imply for cost-per-token

The hardware landscape is more diverse than it has been at any point in the modern LLM era. NVIDIA's Blackwell generation (B200 and B300) increased per-GPU memory and bandwidth substantially over Hopper, with the trade-off of higher per-unit cost and continued supply constraints. AMD's MI300X became a credible alternative for inference workloads where its larger HBM capacity gives it an advantage. AWS Trainium2 and Inferentia variants compete primarily on dollars-per-token within a single cloud rather than on peak performance. Specialized inference accelerators — Groq's deterministic-latency LPU architecture and Cerebras's wafer-scale engines among them — push specific corners of the latency-throughput Pareto front, particularly for long-context decode and very-low-latency interactive use cases.

For a working practitioner the implications are pragmatic rather than glamorous. First, do not assume your current inference runtime supports your next hardware target — porting from CUDA to a non-NVIDIA backend is real work, even with mature compiler stacks. Second, the decoupling of training and inference hardware is real: it is increasingly normal to train on one accelerator family and serve on another. Third, cost-per-token at the frontier has been dropping by roughly an order of magnitude every twelve to eighteen months for comparable quality; planning a budget assuming today's prices is a planning error.

Pitfall

Hardware roadmap slides are notoriously aspirational. Treat any specific date or peak-FLOP claim as a working assumption, not a contractual guarantee, and keep your serving stack portable enough to retarget without rewriting application code.

AI compilers and inference compilers

Underneath every production inference stack is a compiler. Most practitioners ignore this layer until they hit a kernel that nobody has written, a quantization scheme that nobody has fused, or a hardware target that nobody has supported — and then it becomes the only thing that matters. The space is consolidating around a handful of approaches: OpenAI Triton for hand-written GPU kernels at a Python-level abstraction; TVM and the MLIR ecosystem for cross-target compilation pipelines that lower a graph representation through progressively lower-level dialects; Mojo as an emerging systems language aimed at hardware portability for ML primitives; and vendor-specific compilers (CUDA's PTX, ROCm, Neuron) under everything else.

The trend that matters in 2026 is the slow but real arrival of portable, declarative kernel definitions: a single Triton or Mojo source can target multiple backends, fused attention kernels are no longer the property of a single library, and the gap between vendor-supplied operators and best-known operators is narrowing. For practitioners this means two things. One, learning a small amount of GPU programming pays off — even reading kernel source is now a feasible debugging skill, not arcane knowledge. Two, the once-rigid choice between PyTorch eager mode, torch.compile, and a third-party serving runtime is becoming a softer trade-off as compiler stacks converge.

World models and multimodal generalists

The frontier of "what an LLM is" continues to expand. Models that natively process and generate text, images, audio, and video — and reason about three-dimensional scenes, physical dynamics, and embodied action — are moving from research curiosities to deployable systems. World models, in the technical sense of learned simulators that predict next-state given action, are increasingly used as planning substrates for agents that operate in the real world (or in faithful digital twins of it).

For applied engineering this is mostly a long-term signal rather than an immediate planning concern. The systems that will matter in 2027-2028 are the ones being trained now; the systems that matter in 2026 are still primarily text-and-tool-driven. The right posture is to keep your application architecture modality-agnostic — typed message schemas, clean separation between content and reasoning — so that adding a multimodal capability later is a matter of plugging in a new model, not rewriting the application.

What to take away

Three threads run through every trend above. First, the boundary between "model capability" and "system engineering" keeps moving downward into the model — what required scaffolding in 2023 (chain-of-thought prompting, manual tool selection, retrieval orchestration) is increasingly built into the model itself. Second, the pieces of the stack that remain firmly the practitioner's responsibility — evaluation, observability, cost control, safety, data governance — are now where most of the differentiating engineering work happens. Third, the rate of change has not slowed; what has changed is that the set of people who can productively keep up has grown, because the abstractions are finally good enough.

PART 14

Curated resources

A short, deliberately incomplete reading list — chosen for staying power rather than novelty.

This is not a complete bibliography. It is a small, curated set of papers, books, blogs, and code repositories that have proven durable references for practitioners working on real systems. Each entry is annotated with a single line on why it is worth your attention; readers should locate the original sources directly rather than relying on any link.

Foundational papers

These ten papers are the ones most often cited in the back-and-forth of design decisions on production LLM systems. Read them in roughly the order presented if you are starting fresh; otherwise dip in as the situation demands.

Architecture and scaling

  • Attention Is All You Need. The transformer paper. Despite eight years of follow-up, the core architecture and the framing of self-attention as a primitive operation still anchor every modern LLM.
  • Mixture of Experts / Switch Transformer. Sparsely-activated models that route tokens to a subset of experts. Foundational for understanding the design behind every modern MoE architecture, including the trade-offs in routing stability and load balancing.
  • Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces. Selective state-space models as a credible non-attention alternative for long sequences. Worth reading even if you never deploy one — the framing of attention's quadratic cost as a design choice rather than a law of nature is illuminating.

Inference and serving

  • FlashAttention. Memory-efficient exact attention via tiling and recomputation. The single most influential systems paper of the modern LLM era, and a pre-requisite for understanding why long-context inference is even tractable.
  • Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention (vLLM). The paper introducing virtual-memory-style management of the KV cache. Required reading for anyone operating an inference service at scale.

Adaptation and post-training

  • LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models. Frozen base weights plus low-rank update matrices. The dominant parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique and the conceptual foundation for the entire PEFT family.
  • QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs. 4-bit quantized base model with LoRA adapters; the paper that made fine-tuning seven-to-thirteen-billion-parameter models on a single consumer GPU realistic.
  • Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). A reformulation of preference learning that bypasses the explicit reward model required by classical RLHF, replacing it with a closed-form contrastive objective. The de facto first choice for preference-based alignment in 2026.

Retrieval-augmented generation

  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks. The paper that named the technique. Worth reading both for the original framing and as a baseline against which every later RAG variant — including the one in your own codebase — is measured.
  • Self-RAG: Learning to Retrieve, Generate, and Critique through Self-Reflection. An RAG variant in which the model itself decides when to retrieve, what to retrieve, and whether to trust the result. Important for understanding the broader move toward retrieval as a model-driven decision rather than an unconditional pre-step.
Senior tip

Re-read the originals every two or three years. The field's collective memory tends to compress papers into a one-line summary that misses the actual contribution; a fresh reading regularly reveals an idea you can apply that the conventional wisdom has forgotten.

Books worth reading

Three books in particular survive the two-year-shelf-life test that affects most ML writing. None is exclusively about LLMs; that is precisely why they remain useful.

  • Designing Machine Learning Systems · Chip Huyen. The clearest available treatment of ML systems as production engineering — data, training, deployment, monitoring, and the organizational realities that surround each. Most chapters apply to LLM systems with very little translation.
  • Machine Learning Engineering · Andriy Burkov. A practical, opinionated handbook on the full lifecycle of an ML project. The framing of evaluation, error analysis, and deployment risk is durable; the specific algorithms covered are less central than the engineering discipline the book teaches.
  • Hands-On Large Language Models · Jay Alammar & Maarten Grootendorst. A practitioner-facing book that walks through tokenization, embeddings, fine-tuning, RAG, and agents with a clear, visual style. A solid linear path through the LLM stack for engineers approaching it for the first time, and a useful second-pass reference even for experienced practitioners.

Practitioner blogs

The signal-to-noise ratio of LLM writing online is poor. The following authors have each published multi-year archives of consistently high-quality, technically accurate posts that have aged well.

  • Lilian Weng. Long-form survey posts on agent design, prompt engineering, attention variants, and reinforcement learning from human feedback. The single most useful starting point for catching up on a topic you have not been following closely; the posts read like literature reviews written by someone who has actually built the systems.
  • Sebastian Raschka. Detailed, code-grounded explanations of model architectures, fine-tuning techniques, and post-training methods. Strong on rigor and on showing the actual implementation, not just the diagram.
  • Eugene Yan. Practical writing on applied ML systems, evaluation, and the engineering realities of putting LLMs into products. Particularly strong on patterns and anti-patterns in production deployments.

Open-source repositories worth reading code for

The fastest way to internalize how a technique actually works is to read a clean implementation of it. The following repositories are listed by category and are chosen because their codebases are readable, well-organized, and representative of the technique in question.

Pitfall

Reading library documentation is not a substitute for reading library source. The published API often hides the assumption that explains a confusing behavior; the code does not.

How to keep up without drowning

Three lightweight habits go a long way. One, pick three or four primary sources — a paper feed, two blogs, and one curated newsletter — and ignore the rest by default. Two, allocate one hour a week to scanning release notes for the runtimes and frameworks you actually depend on; that catches the changes that will affect you before the social-media wave does. Three, read at least one full paper a month rather than ten abstracts. The signal you are looking for in 2026 is not "what was announced this week" — it is "what is actually being built that we will still be using in 2028".

PART 15

Glossary

Plain-English definitions of the terms used across all five volumes — designed for one-line lookup, not deep explanation.

This glossary covers approximately seventy terms used across Volumes 1-5. Each definition is one line and intentionally compressed; consult the relevant Part for context. Terms are sorted alphabetically, ignoring case and punctuation.

ALiBi
Attention with Linear Biases — encodes position by adding a distance-dependent bias to attention scores rather than via positional vectors.
ANN index
Approximate-nearest-neighbor data structure (e.g., HNSW, IVF) used to make embedding-based retrieval fast on large corpora.
Attention
The transformer mechanism that lets each token weight every other token in its context when producing its representation.
Bi-encoder
A retrieval model that encodes the query and the document independently and compares their vectors with a cheap similarity function.
BPE
Byte-Pair Encoding — a subword tokenization algorithm that iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent symbol pair.
Catastrophic forgetting
The loss of previously acquired capabilities when a model is further trained on a narrower distribution.
Causal LM
A language model that predicts the next token given all preceding tokens, with a causal mask preventing attention to future positions.
Chinchilla scaling
The compute-optimal scaling regime in which training tokens grow roughly proportionally with parameter count.
Chunking
The process of splitting a document into retrieval-sized passages, balancing semantic coherence against retrieval granularity.
ColPali
A late-interaction retrieval model that operates over rendered page images, enabling RAG over visually-rich documents without OCR.
Constitutional AI
An alignment training method that uses a written set of principles plus model-generated critiques and revisions in place of human preference data.
Contextual retrieval
An RAG technique that prepends a model-generated, document-level context summary to each chunk before embedding it.
Continuous batching
An inference scheduling strategy that adds and removes requests from an in-flight batch at every decode step to maximize GPU utilization.
Cross-encoder
A reranking model that takes a (query, document) pair as a single concatenated input and produces a joint relevance score.
DAPT
Domain-Adaptive Pre-Training — continued pre-training of a base model on a domain-specific corpus before instruction tuning.
DDP
Distributed Data Parallel — replicate-the-model, shard-the-batch training; the simplest and still most common multi-GPU training mode.
Disaggregated prefill / decode
An inference architecture that runs the prefill (long prompt) and decode (token-by-token generation) phases on separately specialized instances.
Distillation
Training a smaller "student" model to match the outputs (or internal distributions) of a larger "teacher" model.
DPO
Direct Preference Optimization — preference learning via a contrastive log-likelihood objective, without an explicit reward model.
Drift
Distribution shift in inputs, outputs, or labels over time; a primary driver of silent quality regressions in production.
EAGLE
A speculative-decoding method that uses a lightweight draft head sharing the base model's hidden states to propose multiple tokens per step.
Embedding
A dense vector representation of a piece of text, image, or other input, used for similarity search and downstream classification.
Eval harness
A standardized framework that runs a fixed set of benchmarks against a model and reports comparable scores.
FAISS
An open-source library for efficient vector similarity search, providing a wide range of approximate-nearest-neighbor indexes.
FinOps (LLM)
Financial operations practices applied to LLM workloads — cost attribution, budget guardrails, and unit-economics tracking per request.
FlashAttention
An IO-aware exact-attention kernel that tiles the attention computation to keep operations in fast on-chip memory.
FSDP
Fully Sharded Data Parallel — shards parameters, gradients, and optimizer state across devices to enable training of very large models.
GGUF
A single-file binary format for quantized LLM weights, designed for CPU and on-device inference, originating in the llama.cpp ecosystem.
GPTQ / AWQ
Two widely used post-training weight-quantization methods for LLMs, trading a small quality loss for substantial memory and latency gains.
GraphRAG
A retrieval pattern that constructs a knowledge graph from a corpus and queries the graph (or its summaries) instead of, or alongside, raw chunks.
GQA
Grouped-Query Attention — a middle ground between multi-head and multi-query attention that reduces KV-cache size with minimal quality cost.
Guardrails
Input and output filters that enforce policy, safety, or schema constraints around LLM calls in a production application.
Hallucination
A plausible-sounding but incorrect or unsupported model output; the dominant failure mode in knowledge-intensive applications.
HNSW
Hierarchical Navigable Small World — a graph-based approximate-nearest-neighbor index with strong recall and latency characteristics in practice.
HyDE
Hypothetical Document Embeddings — a query-expansion technique where the model writes a hypothetical answer and the answer's embedding is used for retrieval.
IFEval
An instruction-following benchmark whose tasks are easy to grade automatically because each is paired with a verifiable constraint.
Instruction tuning
Fine-tuning a base language model on instruction-response pairs so that it answers questions rather than continuing arbitrary text.
KV cache
The cached key and value tensors from previously processed tokens, reused across decode steps to avoid recomputing attention from scratch.
Late interaction
A retrieval style (notably ColBERT) that compares every query token against every document token, scoring at the token level rather than the sentence level.
LLM-as-judge
The practice of using a strong LLM to score outputs from other LLMs against a rubric, often as a substitute for human evaluation.
LoRA / QLoRA
Low-Rank Adaptation and its 4-bit-quantized variant — parameter-efficient fine-tuning by training small low-rank update matrices over frozen base weights.
Lost-in-the-middle
The empirical observation that LLMs answer questions more accurately when the relevant context appears at the beginning or end of the prompt rather than the middle.
Mamba / SSM
State-space-model architectures that process sequences with linear-in-length cost, presented as an alternative or complement to attention.
MCP
Model Context Protocol — an open JSON-RPC standard for exposing tools, resources, and prompts to LLM-driven host applications.
MMLU
Massive Multitask Language Understanding — a multi-subject question-answering benchmark widely used for general-knowledge model comparisons.
MoE
Mixture of Experts — a sparse architecture in which a router selects a subset of expert subnetworks per token, increasing capacity without proportionally increasing compute.
MQA
Multi-Query Attention — a variant of attention with a single shared key/value projection across all heads, dramatically shrinking the KV cache.
MTEB
Massive Text Embedding Benchmark — a multi-task suite for evaluating embedding models across retrieval, classification, clustering, and more.
Multi-vector retrieval
Retrieval strategies that represent each document with multiple vectors (per chunk, per sentence, or per token) rather than a single embedding.
NTK scaling
Neural-Tangent-Kernel-aware scaling of rotary position embeddings, used to extend a model's effective context length without full retraining.
Paged attention
A KV-cache management scheme that allocates the cache in fixed-size blocks (pages), enabling near-zero fragmentation under continuous batching.
PEFT
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning — the family of techniques (LoRA, prefix tuning, IA³, etc.) that adapt a base model by training a small fraction of parameters.
Perplexity
The exponential of average per-token cross-entropy loss; an intrinsic measure of how well a language model predicts a held-out corpus.
Prompt caching
Reusing the precomputed prefill (KV cache) of a shared prompt prefix across many requests, drastically lowering per-request cost and latency.
Prompt injection
An adversarial input crafted to override the application's system instructions, often by impersonating a higher-authority message inside untrusted content.
Prompt registry
A versioned, governed store of prompts and templates that decouples prompt content from application code and supports auditing and rollback.
Quantization
Reducing the numeric precision of weights and/or activations (commonly to 8-bit or 4-bit) to lower memory and accelerate inference.
RAG
Retrieval-Augmented Generation — an architecture that retrieves relevant documents at inference time and conditions the LLM on them as context.
RAGAs
A reference-free evaluation framework for RAG systems, scoring faithfulness, answer relevance, and context precision via LLM-as-judge.
Reranker
A model (typically a cross-encoder) that re-scores the top-k results from an initial retriever to improve the final ordering before the LLM sees them.
Reward hacking
The exploitation of unintended shortcuts in a reward signal, producing high reward without satisfying the underlying intent.
RLHF
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — alignment training using a reward model trained on human preference comparisons, optimized via policy gradient methods.
RoPE
Rotary Position Embedding — encodes position by rotating query and key vectors in a way that makes attention scores depend on relative position.
Self-RAG
An RAG variant in which the model itself emits special tokens deciding when to retrieve, what to retrieve, and whether to trust the retrieved evidence.
SFT
Supervised Fine-Tuning — training a base or instruction-tuned model on curated input-output pairs with standard cross-entropy loss.
SimPO
Simple Preference Optimization — a reference-model-free preference-learning objective in the DPO family, often simpler and cheaper to train.
Speculative decoding
An inference technique in which a small draft model proposes several future tokens and the large model verifies them in a single forward pass.
SwiGLU
A gated feed-forward variant using the SiLU/Swish activation; widely adopted in modern transformer blocks for its empirical quality gains.
Test-time compute
The inference-time reasoning budget — how many tokens or steps the model is allowed to spend before producing a final answer.
TGI
Text Generation Inference — a production-oriented LLM serving runtime with continuous batching, streaming, and quantization support.
vLLM
A high-throughput LLM inference and serving engine built around paged attention and continuous batching.
YaRN
"Yet another RoPE extensioN" — a long-context extension method that combines NTK-aware interpolation with attention-temperature scaling.
ZeRO
A family of memory optimizers (stages 1-3) that shard optimizer state, gradients, and parameters across data-parallel ranks to enable training of very large models.

If a term in another Part is unfamiliar, this is the first place to look; if a term is missing here, it is either too narrow to belong in a series glossary or new enough that its definition has not yet stabilized. The next edition will retire entries whose techniques have been superseded and add the ones that the next two years prove durable.
